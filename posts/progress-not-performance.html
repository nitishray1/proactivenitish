The first habit to build when you're exhausted

Not the most impressive habit. Not the one that makes a good caption. The one that requires zero conditions to be right before you can do it.

💧
Nitish Ray Proactive Nitish · Sydney, Australia

People often come into a first conversation expecting me to give them a list of things to start doing. A morning routine. A nutrition overhaul. A movement plan. Something that looks and feels like a real change.

What I actually give most people, at least to start with, is one thing. And it's almost always embarrassingly small.

Why the first habit has to be boring

The purpose of the first habit isn't to produce dramatic results. The purpose is to prove to yourself that you can show up — consistently, even on bad days — before adding anything else on top.

Most wellness plans fail not because the habits are wrong, but because too many are introduced at once. The cognitive load of tracking multiple new behaviours is itself exhausting. It takes willpower, which is a finite resource, especially at the end of a long workday.

The first habit should require almost no willpower. It should be so small, so frictionless, and so clearly defined that doing it feels almost trivial. Because the goal isn't the habit itself — it's the consistency underneath it.

One thing done every single day for thirty days is worth more than ten things done with enthusiasm for a week and abandoned.

The habit I recommend most often — and why

Drink a glass of water before you check your phone in the morning.

That's it. I know how it sounds. Let me explain why it's not as arbitrary as it seems.

First, it's the first thing in the day, which means it doesn't compete with energy you've already spent. You haven't had the chance to get tired, overwhelmed, or distracted yet.

Second, it requires zero preparation. The glass is in the kitchen. The tap is on the wall. There is no version of this that requires planning the night before or buying special equipment.

Third, mild dehydration after sleep is genuinely common — especially if you had coffee or alcohol the evening before — and it contributes to the foggy, slow feeling many people attribute to "not being a morning person." A glass of water does something real.

Fourth, and most importantly: it anchors the habit to an existing behaviour (checking your phone) in a way that makes forgetting it nearly impossible. The phone is going to happen. The water just has to happen first.

✅ The test for a good first habit

Can you still do it on the worst day of your week — exhausted, behind schedule, not in the mood? If the answer is yes, it's a good first habit. If the answer is "not on a bad day", it's not robust enough yet. Make it smaller.

What to do after the first habit is solid

Wait. Genuinely wait. Most people want to add the second thing after a week. The second thing should wait until the first thing is so automatic it doesn't require any thought at all. That usually takes three to four weeks.

When the first habit is solid — when you've stopped thinking about whether to do it and just do it — that's when you add one more thing. Just one. And you apply the same test: can I do this on a bad day?

This is slow. It feels slower than it should. But compounded over six months, it produces a fundamentally different outcome than ten habits introduced at once and abandoned after ten days.

🔑 Key ideas from this post
  • The first habit's job is to prove you can show up consistently — not to produce dramatic results
  • It must survive your worst day, not just your best one
  • Anchor new habits to existing behaviours to make forgetting them harder
  • Wait until the first habit is automatic before adding a second one
  • Slow stacking produces lasting change. Fast stacking produces abandoned plans.

If you want to figure out what your first habit should actually be — based on your schedule, your energy patterns, and where you're most likely to show up — that's exactly the kind of thing the first conversation is for.
